The Northgate Institute for Advanced Materials (NIAM) closed its fifth full year of operation in December, and this report records what the institute attempted, what it achieved, and where it fell short. NIAM was founded to bring together people who grow crystals, people who build the electronics that measure them, and people who write the software that makes sense of the resulting data. The founding argument was simple: a discovery in materials science is rarely held up by a lack of ideas, but very often by the slow hand-offs between specialists who rarely speak to each other. Placing the three kinds of expertise under one roof, on one network, and in one budget was meant to shorten those hand-offs, and the evidence of the past year suggests the bet is still paying off.

Dr. Priya Raman has served as scientific director of NIAM since 2021. A condensed-matter physicist by training, she chairs the monthly steering meeting, approves the annual plan, and represents the institute in negotiations with funders and partner universities. In the opening pages of last year's report she described the institute's mission as connecting careful laboratory work with reproducible computation, and she repeated that formulation at this year's staff assembly. Raman is quick to point out that she does not run the groups herself. Each of the four groups has its own head, its own budget line, and a great deal of freedom in choosing its projects, and the steering meeting exists mainly to make sure the groups do not drift apart.

The four groups are crystal growth, control systems, machine learning, and operations. Dr. Anika Voss leads crystal growth, Tomas Ekberg leads control systems, and Dr. Jonas Keller leads machine learning. The operations group covers finance, facilities, safety, and outreach, and it is the only group without a single head; its responsibilities are shared among Martina Keller, who manages the finances, Lukas Brandt, who oversees facilities and safety, and Elena Marchetti, who coordinates education and outreach. Martina Keller and Jonas Keller are not related, a point worth stating because visitors regularly assume otherwise and because the shared surname has produced more than one misdirected email.

NIAM does not work alone. The institute maintains a long-standing partnership with the Technical University of Munich (TUM), which supplies several joint doctoral students and hosts NIAM staff for short research stays. A second agreement links the institute to the University of Stuttgart (UoS), chiefly for shared access to large instruments that neither institution could justify buying alone. A third, looser relationship exists with Rheinbach Technical University (RTU), whose engineering faculty sends students to NIAM for summer placements. Throughout this report the institute is referred to as NIAM and its three university partners as TUM, UoS, and RTU, in the shorter forms that staff use among themselves.

The year began with a strategic review. In January the board asked each group head to submit a two-page statement of what the group would stop doing, as well as what it would start. The request caused some discomfort, because research institutes are generally better at accumulating projects than at ending them, but the exercise produced useful results. Voss proposed to retire two older furnaces that consumed a great deal of electricity for modest output. Ekberg proposed to stop maintaining three bespoke data acquisition programs in favour of a single standard framework. Keller proposed to abandon a family of image classifiers that had never moved beyond the prototype stage. All three proposals were accepted, and the freed resources were reallocated within the same groups.

The institute employed sixty-four people at the end of the year, up from fifty-nine twelve months earlier. Of these, twenty-two were doctoral researchers, nine of them on joint contracts with TUM, and six were postdoctoral fellows. The remainder were technicians, engineers, administrators, and student assistants. Gender balance remains uneven in the technical roles, a topic Raman raised directly at the assembly, and the institute has committed to reviewing its recruitment practices before the next round of vacancies is advertised.

Several external events shaped the year. The national research grant that funds a large share of NIAM's personnel was renewed for a further three years after a competitive review, a result that gave the institute welcome stability. A European collaborative grant, awarded jointly with partners in Lisbon and Gothenburg, began its first full year of activity. Meanwhile an industrial partner, Altmark Instruments GmbH, signed a framework agreement for the joint development of a new sample stage, which will be described in the section on control systems. The Hollis Foundation, a private charitable body, continued to fund the institute's school outreach programme for the third year running.

Looking back over the twelve months, Raman characterises the year as one of consolidation rather than expansion. The institute did not launch a spectacular new programme, but it finished things that had been left open, and it laid down the infrastructure for projects that will bear fruit in the coming years. The sections that follow describe the work of each group in turn, then turn to finance, personnel, publications, facilities, outreach, and finally the outlook for the next period. Readers who are interested only in a single group can read its section on its own, although the cross-references will be easier to follow in order.

One practical note on naming. People appear in this report under their full names on first mention and under their surnames or first names afterwards, following the habit of the staff themselves. Instruments and software systems are given short names when they are first introduced, and the short names are used from then on without further explanation. Where a short name has two meanings, as happens occasionally with project codes, the surrounding sentence should make clear which one is intended.

The crystal growth group had a productive year, and the credit belongs largely to its head, Dr. Anika Voss, who spent most of the twelve months refining the growth of bismuth telluride. Bismuth telluride is a layered semiconductor that has attracted attention both as a thermoelectric material and as a candidate host for exotic surface states. The difficulty with it is that small departures from the ideal composition change its electronic behaviour dramatically, so a crystal that looks perfect to the eye may be useless for the physicists waiting to measure it. Voss set out to reduce that variability, and by the end of the year the group could grow crystals several millimetres thick with a composition spread that was roughly half of what it had been in the previous year.

The main tool was the Bridgman furnace, in which a sealed ampoule of molten material is lowered slowly through a temperature gradient so that a single crystal nucleates at the cold end and grows upward. Voss found that the quality of the crystals depended less on the temperature itself than on how smoothly the ampoule moved. A small vibration transmitted through the mounting frame was enough to introduce stacking faults, planes where the layers of the crystal are shifted relative to one another. After the group stiffened the frame and replaced the old drive with a stepper motor controlled by the control systems group, the faults became far rarer. This was one of several places where the cooperation between groups, which the institute was designed to encourage, produced a concrete improvement.

A second line of work concerned strontium titanate, an oxide that serves as a substrate for thin films and that becomes superconducting at very low temperatures when it is slightly doped. Dr. Rahul Menon, a postdoctoral fellow in the group, grew doped crystals by the flux method, in which the material dissolves in a molten salt and crystallises as the mixture cools. Menon is a quiet and meticulous experimentalist who keeps a handwritten logbook in addition to the electronic one, and he credits the logbook with helping him to trace a puzzling contamination to a batch of crucible liners. Once the liners were replaced, the oxygen vacancies that had plagued the first samples dropped sharply, and the resistivity curves began to show the sharp transition that theory predicts.

Menon's samples travelled widely. Some were sent to TUM, where collaborators in the electron microscopy laboratory examined them at atomic resolution, and others went to UoS, where the high-field magnet allowed measurements that NIAM cannot perform itself. The exchange worked in both directions: Menon spent six weeks in Munich in the spring learning a sample preparation technique, and he brought back a protocol that the crystal growth group has since adopted. It is a small example of the kind of knowledge transfer that is hard to arrange through emails and invoices and easy to arrange through a visit.

Yuki Tanabe, a doctoral researcher on a joint contract between NIAM and TUM, joined the group in the autumn of last year. Tanabe completed a bachelor's degree in materials engineering at a university in Osaka, then a master's degree at UoS, where she wrote her thesis on the growth of oxide thin films. Her work at NIAM concerns lithium niobate, a crystal prized for its optical properties and notoriously prone to inhomogeneities. Tanabe built a small optical floating-zone furnace, a design in which a focused lamp melts a thin zone of a rod and the zone is moved along the rod to form a single crystal, and she grew her first usable crystals in June. Her supervisor, Voss, describes the speed of the progress as unusual for a first-year student.

Not everything went smoothly. The group's oldest flux-growth oven failed twice, and each failure cost about a week of production while a replacement controller was ordered and fitted. A shipment of high-purity tellurium was held at customs for nearly a month, and Voss was forced to reorganise the growth schedule around the delay. She is blunt about the lesson: the group will in future hold a larger reserve of the raw materials that are hard to replace quickly, even though doing so ties up money that could be spent elsewhere. This decision was supported by the steering meeting after some discussion with the finance manager, who is naturally cautious about inventory.

The group also took a decision to stop growing a family of cuprate crystals that it had worked on since the founding of the institute. The cuprates were historically important, and Voss has a personal fondness for them, but the group's results had stopped being competitive, and several other laboratories now produce better crystals with larger furnaces. The decision was one of the three accepted in the strategic review described earlier. The two retired furnaces, which had run for more than a decade, were dismantled in October, and their power supplies were reused by the control systems group in its test benches.

Documentation received more attention than in earlier years. Every growth run is now recorded in a standard template that captures the starting materials, the temperature profile, the pulling rate, the atmosphere, and the result of the first inspection. Voss insists that the record be completed on the day of the run, not remembered later, and Menon helped design a form that takes only a few minutes to fill in. The records are stored alongside the furnace logs and are available to the machine learning group, which is using them to train models that predict which growth conditions are likely to yield good crystals. Early results of that work are described in the section on machine learning.

Looking forward, the group plans to build a second crystal growth line dedicated to samples for TUM and UoS, so that external orders no longer compete with NIAM's own projects. Voss estimates that the new line would raise the group's output by about a third and would allow Tanabe to move on to a more ambitious set of compounds. Funding for the line is not yet secured, and the outlook section returns to the question of how the institute intends to pay for it. In the meantime, the group will continue to improve its existing process, to publish its growth protocols openly, and to train the next generation of crystal growers, a craft that Voss believes is disappearing from many laboratories.

The control systems group, led by Tomas Ekberg, is responsible for everything that connects an instrument to a computer. When NIAM was founded, each laboratory had its own way of reading a thermometer or moving a motor, and an experiment that crossed two laboratories required someone to translate between incompatible conventions. Ekberg's task, which he describes as unglamorous but essential, has been to replace this patchwork with a single framework. The framework the group chose is the Experimental Physics and Industrial Control System (EPICS), an open-source toolkit originally developed at large accelerator laboratories and now used in research facilities all over the world.

In EPICS, each device is served by a small program called an input-output controller (IOC), which exposes the device's readings and settings as named process variables. Any other program on the network can read or write those variables without knowing anything about the hardware behind them. This year the group wrote new IOCs for the cryostat controller, the vacuum gauge array, and the temperature monitor in the low-temperature laboratory, bringing the number of devices connected to the shared network to just over ninety. Noor Haddad, a control engineer who joined in March, wrote most of the records for the vacuum system and documented every process variable in the institute wiki, a task Ekberg insists on for every new device.

The documentation rule is strict. Before an instrument enters service, it must have a record for each measured quantity, an alarm limit, and a short operating note explaining what the device does and who to call when it misbehaves. Ekberg is aware that engineers find this tedious and that scientists find it irrelevant until the day a pressure gauge fails at two in the morning. In October exactly that happened, and the on-call technician was able to find the right procedure within minutes because the operating note existed. The incident did more to convince the sceptics than any amount of argument.

A persistent problem during the year was intermittent timeouts on the motor stage in the characterisation hall. Haddad spent three weeks chasing it. The symptom was that a scan would run normally for an hour or two and then stall, with the motor controller reporting no error. She eventually traced the fault to an unreliable port on one of the network switches that dropped packets under heavy load, and the replacement of the switch removed the problem completely. The episode also exposed a gap in the network documentation, which was missing a current diagram of how the switches were cabled together. Ekberg assigned Felix Hartmann, a student assistant from RTU, to survey every cable in the laboratory and produce a complete diagram.

The embedded software for the stepper motors was rewritten during the summer. The old firmware, written years earlier by a contractor, moved the motors in coarse steps that caused visible vibration at low speed, and the vibration affected measurements as well as crystal growth. Ekberg and Haddad wrote new firmware that interpolates between steps, which makes slow movements far smoother. The same firmware now drives the ampoule in the Bridgman furnace described in the crystal growth section, which is the improvement that Anika Voss credits with reducing stacking faults. The firmware is stored in the institute repository and can be reused by anyone who needs a smooth, slow drive.

The most visible project of the year was the design of a new sample stage with Altmark Instruments GmbH. The stage holds a sample at a fixed temperature while it is moved through a beam of light, and it must do so with a positioning accuracy of a few micrometres. Altmark contributes the mechanical design and the manufacturing capacity, and NIAM contributes the control electronics and the software. The first prototype arrived in September and was assembled on a test bench by Haddad and Hartmann. Preliminary tests are encouraging, although the thermal drift of the stage is still larger than the specification allows, and the partners have agreed to meet in January to decide whether to change the design or to compensate in software.

The group's relationship with UoS deepened. Engineers from Stuttgart visited in the spring to compare how their instruments are wired and to share lessons about timeouts and misbehaving firmware, and Ekberg spent a week at the Stuttgart facility in return. One practical outcome is a shared library of IOC templates that both institutions now maintain. Ekberg regards the library as a modest success: it contains only a handful of templates, but each one saves a new engineer several days of work.

Operators now see all readbacks of the main laboratories on a single panel, which replaced three older displays that used different colour schemes and different units. Alarms are routed to a shared channel and to the on-call phone, and the number of alarms that required human action fell by a quarter compared with last year, mostly because thresholds were tuned and duplicate alarms removed. The group also keeps spare boards for the most critical controllers on a labelled shelf, a precaution that proved its worth when the cryostat controller failed in November and was swapped in under an hour.

Looking ahead, the group wants to give every instrument at NIAM a uniform interface regardless of its manufacturer, so that the machine learning group can collect data from all of them without writing a separate adapter for each. Ekberg accepts that this goal is ambitious and will take years. His immediate priorities are to finish the Altmark stage, to retire the last three bespoke acquisition programs, and to train two more people in EPICS so that the group's knowledge no longer rests on a single engineer who happens to remember how everything is wired.

The computing infrastructure of the institute is modest by the standards of a large laboratory but is used heavily by every group. The shared cluster consists of twelve nodes, four of which carry graphics processors, and it is administered by Tomas Ekberg's group in addition to its control duties. Users submit jobs through a queue, which allocates resources according to a fair-share rule so that no single group can monopolise the machines. Last year the average utilisation was around seventy percent, and in the weeks before conference deadlines it rose to ninety-five, at which point the queue grew long and tempers grew short.

The network connecting the laboratories, the cluster, and the file server was reorganised in the spring. Previously, instruments, office computers, and servers shared a single flat network, which was simple but allowed a fault in one place to disturb everything. The control systems group divided it into separate segments, with the instruments on one, the servers on a second, and the office machines on a third, and with a firewall between them that permits only the connections that are needed. Noor Haddad wrote the rules and tested each of them, and the cutover took place over a weekend in May. The only surprise was an old oscilloscope that turned out to depend on a connection nobody had documented, and Haddad restored it within the hour.

Security is taken seriously without being allowed to dominate. All staff use individual accounts with strong passwords and a second factor, and administrators use separate accounts for administrative tasks. Software updates are applied monthly on a fixed schedule, with an emergency procedure for urgent patches. The institute's computers have been scanned externally twice, and the scans produced a short list of minor findings, all of which were corrected within a week. Ekberg insists that the biggest risk is not a clever attacker but an unpatched machine in a corner of the laboratory, and he has asked Felix Hartmann to maintain an inventory of every device that connects to the network.

The graphics processors were a point of debate. Dr. Jonas Keller's group would like more of them, and the finance manager has explained that the funder views the cluster as a shared resource and would not support a large purchase for a single group. A compromise was reached under which the four existing processors are reserved for the machine learning group during the night and available to everybody during the day. Hanna Lindqvist, whose language model experiments require long runs, has benefited most from the arrangement, although she admits that it forces her to plan her experiments with unusual care.

Cloud computing was considered and largely rejected. The attractions are obvious: one can rent a powerful machine for a few hours and pay only for what one uses. But the institute's data are voluminous, transferring them costs both time and money, and some of them are subject to confidentiality terms that complicate storage outside the building. Ekberg proposed a rule that cloud services may be used for experiments on public data and for short bursts of capacity, but that confidential or large datasets stay on the institute's own systems. The steering meeting adopted it without much debate.

Software environments are managed with reproducibility in mind. Every project that produces a published result must record the exact versions of its software dependencies, and the institute provides a set of standard environments that users may adopt or extend. Dr. Viktor Sokolov built a template for machine learning projects that fixes the random seeds, logs the configuration of every training run, and stores the trained model alongside the data on which it was trained. Several colleagues from other groups have adopted the template for their own analyses, and Sokolov regards that as the best compliment he could have received.

Monitoring ties all of this together. A dashboard shows the load on the cluster, the free space on the file server, the temperature of the server room, and the status of the backups, and it sends an alert to the on-call phone if any of them leaves its normal range. The thresholds are reviewed every quarter, and the number of false alarms has fallen as the groups have gained experience. In November the dashboard warned that the server room's air conditioning was failing, and the technician who answered the call managed to prevent an overheating incident that would have interrupted all the groups for days.

Looking ahead, Ekberg wishes to replace the oldest four nodes of the cluster, which are approaching the end of their useful life, and to introduce a scheduler that can take account of energy cost as well as demand. The sustainability working group supports the idea, since it would allow the cluster to run heavy jobs when electricity is cheaper and to rest when it is expensive. The proposal will be included in the application for the equipment infrastructure programme, alongside the second crystal growth line, and the steering meeting will decide on the ranking of the two requests in the spring.

The machine learning group, headed by Dr. Jonas Keller, applies statistical methods to the data that the rest of the institute produces. Keller is wary of the word artificial intelligence, which he considers too grand for the quiet, statistical work his group actually does, and he prefers to describe the group as a data analysis service with ambitions. Those ambitions grew this year. The group trained models on three kinds of data: crystal structure records, spectroscopy archives, and the logs that the furnaces and instruments write to disk. It also began a larger project that reads the institute's own scientific literature and organises what it finds.

The first project predicts the stability of candidate compounds. Dr. Viktor Sokolov, a postdoctoral fellow, trained a graph neural network on crystal structure records, representing each crystal as a set of atoms connected by bonds. The model ranks candidate compounds by their likelihood of being stable, and the crystal growth group used its ranking to choose which of several doped compositions to attempt first. Sokolov reports that the model is useful as a filter rather than as an oracle: it removes the worst candidates, but the distinction among the good ones still has to be made in the furnace. Keller makes the same point more bluntly, saying that a model which is right seventy percent of the time is valuable if it saves three failed growth runs.

The second project is the one that engages the whole institute. Hanna Lindqvist, a doctoral researcher on a joint contract with TUM, is building a pipeline that reads scientific papers and turns them into a knowledge graph (KG). A knowledge graph stores facts as small statements of the form subject, relation, object, for example that a particular crystal was grown by a particular method, and links the statements through the entities they share. The attraction for a materials laboratory is obvious: instead of searching a thousand papers for every mention of a compound, a researcher could ask the graph what is known about it and follow the connections from one result to another.

Lindqvist's pipeline has three stages. A language model first splits each document into chunks and extracts candidate facts from each chunk, each with a quotation from the text that supports it. A second pass reviews the extracted facts, merging fragments that belong together and adding facts the first pass missed. Finally, a resolution step decides which names refer to the same entity, so that the Technical University of Munich, TUM, and the shortened form used in a particular paper all end up as a single node. Lindqvist found that this last step was the hardest. Names that look alike are often different entities, and names that look different are often the same, and a wrong merge can corrupt every answer that passes through the merged node.

The group measured the pipeline against a hand-built set of reference facts. Lindqvist and a student assistant annotated forty pages of text, listing every fact a careful reader would want to retain, and the pipeline's output was scored for recall, the fraction of reference facts it found, and for precision, the fraction of its own facts that were correct. The first version found about two thirds of the reference facts, and it invented a small number of facts that the text did not support. A change in the instructions to the model, requiring a verbatim quotation for every fact, eliminated most of the invented facts. Recall improved after the second review pass was added, although the group noticed that long inputs made the model more likely to drop facts from the middle of the text, which argues for smaller chunks.

Keller's group also looked at how the documents are split before extraction. The default approach cuts the text into pieces of a fixed size, preferring to break at paragraph and sentence boundaries. A more elaborate alternative places boundaries where the meaning of consecutive sentences changes, using the similarity of their embeddings. Lindqvist tested both on the reference documents and found small differences that did not justify extra complexity for ordinary prose, although the semantic method did better on a long report that jumped between unrelated topics. The group concluded that the simpler approach is a sound default and that the choice should be revisited only if measurements on a specific collection of documents show a clear gain.

The third project was an anomaly detector for the furnace logs, built by Sokolov in cooperation with the crystal growth group. It learns what a normal temperature profile looks like and raises a flag when a run deviates from it. During testing it correctly flagged a growth run in which the controller had silently lost its calibration, which saved Voss's group from spending two weeks on a worthless crystal. False alarms remain a nuisance, and the group is tuning the thresholds with the help of the technicians who know which deviations matter.

Compute resources were a constraint. The institute's cluster is shared among all groups, and training jobs were scheduled at night to keep the machines free during working hours. Keller has argued for a dedicated machine for the group's language model experiments, and the finance section explains why the request was approved only in part. In the meantime the group relies on small models and on careful engineering, which Keller regards as a healthy discipline.

Data management is the least glamorous part of the institute's work and, in the opinion of several group heads, the part most likely to determine whether NIAM is remembered in ten years. Every instrument at the institute produces files, every file has a story about how it was made, and unless that story is recorded at the time, it is lost within months. The institute therefore adopted a data management policy at the start of the year, drafted by a small working group of Tomas Ekberg, Jonas Keller, and Dr. Rahul Menon, who represented the experimentalists. The policy is two pages long on purpose, since a longer document would not have been read.

The policy has four rules. Raw data are never edited; processed data are always derived from raw data by a script that is stored with the result. Every dataset carries a short description of the instrument, the sample, the date, and the person responsible. Datasets are stored on the institute file server for at least ten years, with a second copy in a separate building. And any dataset behind a published figure must be deposited in an open repository, unless a legal or contractual obligation forbids it. Menon admits that the third rule was greeted with groans, but notes that the second copy saved a week of work in March when a disk array failed and the backup was needed.

The file server was upgraded in the spring. The old machine, installed shortly after the founding of the institute, had reached the end of its warranty, and its capacity was nearly exhausted by the growing volume of microscopy images. The new server holds about four times as much, uses a redundant arrangement of disks that tolerates two failures, and replicates nightly to a machine in a different building on the site. Ekberg's group configured the replication and tested the restoration procedure by deliberately deleting a directory and recovering it, which is the only test of a backup that really counts.

Access control was tightened. Previously, anyone with an account could read almost everything, which was convenient but increasingly uncomfortable as the institute began to host data supplied by industrial partners under confidentiality terms. Each project now has its own folder with a named owner who decides who may read or write, and the owner is reminded every six months to review the list. The change took several weeks to implement and prompted a number of complaints, which Ekberg answered by pointing out that nobody had lost access to anything they actually needed.

Metadata, the descriptive information that makes a dataset interpretable, proved harder than storage. The institute tried a standard template for measurement files that records the instrument, its settings, and the sample identifier, and the control systems group modified the data acquisition software to fill in most fields automatically. Scientists still have to enter the sample identifier by hand, and mistakes in this one field cause the most trouble, because a mislabelled sample cannot be recovered from the data alone. The group is considering barcode labels on sample holders that the software can read directly.

The machine learning group depends on this infrastructure. Jonas Keller points out that his group's models are only as good as the metadata attached to the training examples, and that much of the group's time this year went into cleaning labels, not into designing models. Hanna Lindqvist's literature pipeline depends on a similar discipline: every fact it extracts carries a reference to the document and the passage it came from, so that a researcher who doubts a statement can read the original sentence. The same principle, provenance for every claim, underlies the data policy.

Software is treated as data too. All code written at the institute is kept in a shared repository, and any code used to produce a published result must be tagged at the version used. Lukas Brandt, who oversees facilities, had to be persuaded that this applied to the scripts that run laboratory equipment, but he now agrees that a safety interlock whose code nobody can find is a liability. The repository also holds the firmware for the stepper motors and the templates for the control system, and it has become a de facto record of how the institute's instruments have evolved.

Not every effort succeeded. A proposed institute-wide electronic lab notebook was abandoned after a trial, because the commercial product that was tested did not integrate with the control system and required scientists to enter information twice. Ekberg has suggested building a lightweight alternative on top of the existing wiki, and a student assistant, Felix Hartmann, is investigating whether this is feasible within a few months. Meanwhile, the crystal growth group continues to use its own paper and spreadsheet records, which work but are hard to search.

Looking forward, the working group plans to publish the policy and its supporting templates openly, in the hope that other small institutes will find them useful. Menon, who has become the unofficial ambassador for the policy, reports that the best argument for it has not been a principle but an anecdote: a doctoral student in the spring found, six months after the end of an experiment, that the only copy of an essential calibration file was on a laptop that had been returned to the supplier. The data policy now ensures that such a file lives on the server from the day it is created.

Martina Keller manages the institute's finances and prepares the budget every autumn. Her job, as she describes it, is to make sure that the decisions of the scientists can actually be paid for, and to tell them in good time when they cannot. She is not related to Jonas Keller, the head of the machine learning group, and she has learned to answer the question before it is asked. This year's budget closed at 9.4 million euros, a rise of about six percent over the previous year, and the accounts were reviewed by external auditors who raised no objections, although they recommended clearer documentation of travel claims.

Personnel costs again dominate, accounting for roughly sixty-one percent of expenditure. The remainder is divided between equipment, consumables, travel, and the running costs of the buildings, with the electricity bill alone consuming a larger share than the institute would like. The sustainability section explains what is being done about that. Keller keeps a rolling forecast, updated monthly, which shows commitments against remaining funds, and she brings it to the steering meeting so that Dr. Priya Raman and the group heads can see how a decision in one group affects the others.

The national research grant, which is the institute's largest source of external funding, was renewed this year for three years at 4.2 million euros. The renewal followed a competitive review in which an international panel examined the institute's output and plans, and it was, in Raman's words, the best news of the year. The grant pays for twelve doctoral positions and part of the equipment budget, and it requires annual progress reports, which Keller and the group heads prepare together in the spring. Unspent funds from the previous period were carried forward after approval by the board.

A European collaborative grant, awarded jointly with partners in Lisbon and Gothenburg, brought 1.8 million euros to NIAM over four years. The first full year of the grant paid for three positions and a share of the computing cluster, and the partners met twice in person. The grant requires NIAM to spend part of its share on mobility, sending staff to the partner institutions and hosting their visitors, so it also underwrites the exchanges that the institute values. Keller notes that the reporting rules for European funds are stricter than for national money, and she has trained a student assistant to help with the paperwork.

The industrial framework agreement with Altmark Instruments GmbH is smaller in value, about 380 thousand euros over two years, but it is attractive because the partner contributes manufacturing capacity that NIAM could not afford. Under the agreement, Altmark pays for the mechanical parts of the new sample stage and NIAM pays for the control electronics. Keller negotiated a clause giving NIAM the right to publish the scientific results obtained with the stage, a point on which she was unwilling to compromise. The Hollis Foundation continued its support of the school outreach programme with a grant of 120 thousand euros, spent largely on materials and on the wages of the student helpers who run the demonstrations.

Equipment purchases above a fixed threshold require three quotations, which Keller collects and files with the order. This year the largest purchase was a replacement cryostat for the low-temperature laboratory, delivered in the autumn after a delay of nearly three months. The delay pushed part of the planned spending into the following financial year, and Keller had to arrange the carry-over with the funder. She also negotiated a favourable maintenance contract for the electron microscope at TUM that the institute uses by arrangement, reducing the running costs by a noticeable margin.

The machine learning group asked for a dedicated server with several graphics processors to train larger language models. The steering meeting approved half of the request. Keller explained that the other half would have been difficult to justify to the national funder, who regards the cluster as a shared resource, and that she preferred to keep the proposal modest and to revisit it if measurements showed that the shared cluster was a bottleneck. Jonas Keller accepted the decision with good grace, although he pointed out that his group had spent a considerable fraction of its time waiting for jobs to run.

Risks to the budget are mostly external. Energy prices could rise further, and delays in equipment deliveries make it hard to spend funds within the period for which they were granted. A more subtle risk is the dependence on a small number of large grants: if the national grant were not renewed in three years, the institute would have to shrink quickly. Keller therefore recommended building a modest reserve and raising the share of third-party income, and the board agreed to set aside a fixed percentage of unrestricted funds each year for this purpose.

Looking ahead to next year, Keller expects a flat budget in real terms and a small deficit in the first half, caused by the timing of the cryostat payments and the renovation described in the facilities section. She is confident that the deficit will close by autumn, provided the European grant's second instalment arrives on schedule. Her final message to the group heads is characteristically dry: a good plan is one that survives contact with the invoices.

Staff turnover remained low, which Raman credits to the supportive atmosphere and the freedom that the group heads give their staff. The doctoral programme is the part of the institute's work that is least visible in annual statistics and most important for its future. Of the sixty-four people employed at NIAM at the end of the year, twenty-two were doctoral researchers, and nine of them held joint contracts with TUM. The programme is coordinated by Dr. Anika Voss, who took over from her predecessor three years ago and who regards the task as a duty to the profession. She holds an annual meeting with every doctoral researcher to discuss progress, plans, and any problems with supervision, and she has the authority to arrange a change of supervisor if the relationship breaks down.

Admission follows a standard route. Candidates apply with a master's degree, a statement of research interests, and two references, and a panel of group heads interviews the shortlisted applicants. Yuki Tanabe, who joined the crystal growth group in the autumn of last year, was selected from forty-one applications for her position. The panel valued the combination of her work on oxide thin films during her master's studies at UoS and her evident willingness to build apparatus with her own hands. She describes the interview as the first time anyone had asked her to explain a failed experiment, and she remembers it as a pleasant surprise.

Every doctoral researcher has a primary supervisor and a second advisor from a different group. The second advisor is not a bureaucratic formality: at NIAM the arrangement is intended to ensure that a student from the crystal growth group hears the views of a control engineer, and a student from the machine learning group hears those of an experimentalist. Hanna Lindqvist, whose supervisor is Dr. Jonas Keller, has Voss as her second advisor, and she says that the questions Voss asks are the ones a reviewer would ask. Students meet both advisors at least once every six months in a formal progress review.

Training goes beyond research. All new doctoral researchers attend a course on good scientific practice, a course on scientific writing, and a safety course. They are encouraged to take electives in other disciplines, and the programme pays for external courses up to a fixed amount each year. In the spring, five students attended a course on project management organised by TUM, and two of them reported that it had changed how they plan their experiments. The institute also offers an optional course in German for international students, taught by a volunteer from the administrative staff, and it has been popular.

The weekly journal club is the most beloved institution of the programme. One student presents a recent paper, the others criticise it, and the senior staff stay silent until the end, a rule that Voss introduced to prevent the discussion from being dominated by seniority. The club has produced more than one collaboration between groups, and last winter it led to the idea of using the machine learning group's anomaly detector on the furnace logs. Students rotate through the role of chair, and Tanabe was the first first-year student to chair a session, an experience she calls terrifying and indispensable in equal measure.

The programme also tracks what its graduates do. Of the eleven doctoral researchers who have completed their degrees since the founding of the institute, five took academic positions, four moved into industry, and two founded a company that builds measurement software. The institute regards all three outcomes as successes, although the steering meeting noted that the proportion going into academia is lower than the funders sometimes expect. Voss argues that a trained experimentalist is valuable wherever precise measurement matters, and she cites the alumni network as evidence that the institute's graduates are in demand.

Not all students finish. Two researchers left the programme during the year, one for health reasons and one to take a position in industry. The institute offers support for students in difficulty, including a confidential discussion with the ombudsperson and, where appropriate, an extension of the contract. Voss believes that early warning is the best protection against dropout, and she has asked supervisors to raise concerns at the first sign of trouble rather than waiting for the formal review. She also noted that the pressure of a short contract is a real problem, and she supports the call for longer minimum contract durations.

For the next year, the programme will recruit four new students: two funded by the national grant, one by the European grant, and one in cooperation with TUM. Voss would like to introduce a short exchange period for every student, spent at a partner laboratory, and to make a visit to a conference a standard part of the curriculum. She also wants to publish a handbook for new students, collecting the practical advice that is currently passed on by word of mouth, and she has recruited Tanabe and Lindqvist to write the first draft.

NIAM researchers published eleven papers during the year, several of them with partners at TUM, UoS, and the institutions in Lisbon and Gothenburg. The number is similar to last year, but the group heads agree that the quality has risen, and two of the papers appeared in journals that the institute had not previously reached. Dr. Priya Raman is wary of counting papers as a measure of success, and she asks reviewers to read a few of them instead of tallying the list, but she acknowledges that publication remains the main way in which the institute's work reaches the wider community.

The crystal growth group contributed three papers. The first, led by Dr. Anika Voss, described the improvement in the growth of bismuth telluride that resulted from smoother ampoule motion, and it included a frank discussion of the stacking faults that earlier methods had produced. The second, by Dr. Rahul Menon and collaborators at UoS, reported the superconducting transition in doped strontium titanate crystals grown by the flux method. The third was a methods article in which Voss and Yuki Tanabe set out the growth protocol for lithium niobate in enough detail for another laboratory to reproduce it, a type of paper that she considers undervalued and that she encourages her students to write.

The control systems group published two papers, one at an international instrumentation conference and one in a technical journal. Tomas Ekberg presented the institute's approach to integrating laboratory hardware into EPICS, and described the documentation rule that requires a record, an alarm limit, and an operating note for every device. Noor Haddad co-authored a short paper on the diagnosis of intermittent network faults, based on the investigation of the failing switch port. Both papers attracted questions from engineers at other facilities who face the same problems and who are rarely able to read about them in the scientific literature.

The machine learning group produced three papers. Dr. Viktor Sokolov published the work on the graph neural network that ranks candidate compounds by their likelihood of being stable, and Dr. Jonas Keller co-authored a paper on anomaly detection in furnace logs. The third was a preprint from Hanna Lindqvist on the knowledge graph pipeline, a paper submitted by Raman and her colleagues which was written primarily by Lindqvist. The preprint describes the quotation requirement for extracted facts, the second review pass, and the difficulties of resolving entity names, and it drew interest from groups working on related problems, leading to two new collaborations.

Conferences took up much of the calendar. Ekberg presented at the instrumentation conference in Hamburg, Lindqvist at a workshop on scientific machine learning in Vienna, and Voss at the annual physics congress, where the poster of the crystal growth group won a prize chosen by the attendees. Tanabe gave her first international talk at a summer school in Gothenburg, which she describes as terrifying and indispensable in equal measure. The institute pays for one conference trip per researcher per year and requires the traveller to report back at the internal seminar, so that the whole institute benefits from the journey.

The institute encourages open access, and all accepted papers are deposited in the institutional repository. Authors are asked to share the data and code behind each paper whenever licences allow, in line with the data management policy described earlier. Menon, who helped to draft that policy, noted that the deposit of data had already paid off: a group in another country reanalysed his published resistivity curves and found a feature that he had overlooked, and the resulting correspondence led to a joint study.

Peer review is a duty as well as a privilege. Raman estimated that NIAM staff reviewed around forty manuscripts for journals and conferences during the year. Voss reviewed for a leading materials journal and Keller for several machine learning venues, and both emphasise that they accept only requests within their competence. The institute has also begun to discuss how it can recognise the work of reviewing, which is invisible in most evaluations, and the steering meeting agreed to mention it in the annual report from now on.

Citations to earlier NIAM papers continued to grow steadily, particularly for the paper on control software for cryostats, which has become a modest reference for small laboratories. Not every paper succeeded. A manuscript on thermoelectric measurements was rejected by two journals before being accepted by a third, and the group concluded that the first submission had overstated the novelty of the result. Voss regards the rejection as useful, because the reviewers' comments led to a cleaner experiment and a more honest abstract.

For the coming year, each group head has identified two papers that they intend to complete. Voss plans a follow-up on lithium niobate with the new furnace line, if it is funded. Ekberg wants to describe the Altmark sample stage once the thermal drift is understood. Keller and Lindqvist intend to publish the evaluation of their knowledge graph pipeline on a larger reference set, including a comparison of chunking strategies. The steering meeting reminded everyone that the institute's open access policy applies to preprints as well as to final papers.

The characterisation hall houses the instruments that tell the institute what its crystals actually are. The principal instrument is a single-crystal X-ray diffractometer, which determines the arrangement of atoms in a crystal by measuring how it scatters a beam of X-rays. Every crystal grown at NIAM passes through it before it is cut or measured. Dr. Anika Voss's group uses it to check that a crystal is truly single, to find the orientation of its axes, and to detect the twin boundaries and stacking faults that spoil a sample. Samuel Achterberg, before his retirement, built the mount that holds the crystals, and the group has not yet dared to replace it.

A powder diffractometer serves a related purpose. When a growth run produces only a mass of tiny grains instead of a single crystal, the powder diffractometer identifies which compounds are present, and it has diagnosed more than one failed growth. Menon has used it extensively to study the phases that form when strontium titanate is doped, and he notes that a pattern which looks identical to the untrained eye can conceal a small second phase that changes the electrical behaviour entirely. The instrument is booked less heavily than the single-crystal machine, and Lukas Brandt's new policy releases its idle afternoon slots to other groups.

Electron microscopy is not performed at NIAM itself but at TUM, under the terms of the partnership agreement. Samples are shipped by courier in protective holders, examined at atomic resolution, and returned with images and a short report. The microscope in Munich can resolve individual atomic columns, and it has revealed oxygen vacancies and dislocations that no other instrument available to the institute could detect. The cost of using it is covered by the agreement, but Martina Keller reminds the groups that every hour of microscope time represents a commitment by a colleague in Munich, and that requests should therefore be prepared carefully.

Electrical and magnetic properties are measured in the low-temperature laboratory, in the cryostat whose controller failed in November. It cools samples to a fraction of a kelvin above absolute zero, where superconductivity and magnetic order appear, and it can apply a modest magnetic field. For measurements that need a larger field, samples are taken to the high-field magnet at UoS. The two facilities complement each other: NIAM's cryostat is available at short notice and allows long, patient measurements, while Stuttgart's magnet is booked months ahead but reaches fields that NIAM cannot approach.

Optical measurements are performed by the group of Clara Dubois in Gothenburg, and the equipment at NIAM for such work is limited to a small spectrometer in the sample preparation room. The spectrometer measures how a crystal absorbs light of different colours, which reveals the energy gaps of semiconductors such as bismuth telluride. Yuki Tanabe has used it to check the optical quality of her lithium niobate crystals, and she found that a faint absorption band correlated with the presence of iron impurities. The observation led her to change the supplier of one of her starting materials, with a measurable improvement.

Maintenance of the instruments is a continuous task. Each device has a logbook, a service schedule, and a named responsible person. The X-ray tubes of the diffractometers have a limited lifetime and are replaced when their intensity drops below a threshold, and the cryostat's compressor is serviced every six months. Haddad connected the principal instruments to the monitoring dashboard this year, so that a drop in intensity or a rise in compressor temperature can be seen before it causes a failure. The technicians welcome the dashboard, although some of them find it difficult to accept advice from a screen.

Calibration deserves a special mention. A measurement made with an uncalibrated instrument is not a measurement but an opinion, as Ekberg likes to say, and the institute keeps a record of the calibration history of every device. The discovery in Gothenburg that some old spectra had been recorded with a different calibration than the metadata claimed reinforced the point. The institute now requires that each measurement file carries the identifier of the calibration it relied on, and that the calibration records are stored with the data. The requirement forms part of the data management policy and was extended in the autumn to cover the instruments of visiting researchers.

Lukas Brandt oversees the facilities of NIAM and coordinates safety training. He joined the institute from a chemical company where he held a similar post, and he brings a manner that colleagues describe as courteous and immovable. The institute occupies two floors of a purpose-built laboratory building on the university campus, together with part of the basement where the heaviest equipment is housed. Brandt's responsibility covers the building services, the laboratory spaces, the handling of hazardous materials, and the safety of everyone who works there, from the director to the newest student assistant.

The cleanroom was refurbished during the year, which was the largest facilities project in the institute's history. The old ventilation system was noisy, uneven in its airflow, and expensive to run. Replacing it required the cleanroom to close for three weeks in July, a period that Brandt planned months in advance in consultation with the groups that depend on it. The new system filters the air more effectively, uses roughly a third less electricity, and includes additional gas sensors that warn automatically if a concentration of a hazardous gas rises above the limit. After reopening, the room was recertified by an external inspector, and the first samples were processed within two days.

The low-temperature laboratory received a new cryostat in the autumn, after the delivery delay described in the finance section. Cryogenic liquids such as liquid helium and liquid nitrogen are central to the institute's measurements and are also among its principal hazards. They can displace oxygen in a closed room, and a spill causes severe cold burns. Work with these liquids follows written procedures that every user must read and sign, and the laboratory is equipped with oxygen monitors that sound an alarm at a safe margin above the dangerous level. Brandt insists that nobody should work alone with cryogenic liquids, a rule that he enforces with unfailing politeness.

An incident in September led to a new checklist. A doctoral student, working late, opened a nitrogen dewar without the usual precautions and was splashed on the hand. The injury was minor and the student recovered within days, but Brandt treated it as a serious warning. He interviewed the student and the colleagues who had seen the event, concluded that the procedure was clear but the equipment was awkward, and replaced the dewar's lid with a safer design. He also introduced a short checklist that is posted at the door of the low-temperature laboratory and takes less than a minute to read. Since then, no further incident has been recorded.

Emergency drills are held twice a year, and this year the evacuation route from the characterisation hall was marked more clearly after the drill showed that visitors could not find the exit. Brandt also arranged for the old chemicals that had accumulated in storage to be removed by a licensed contractor, a task that had been postponed for years. Thirty-one containers were taken away, some of them labelled in a handwriting that nobody could identify. The inventory of hazardous chemicals is now maintained in a shared spreadsheet that the groups update whenever they buy or use a substance.

The roof above the sample preparation room leaked during the autumn storms, and water damaged a ceiling panel and a cabinet of consumables. Brandt negotiated with the university, which owns the building, to have the roof repaired, and he secured a commitment to inspect all of the roof joints before winter. He regards the incident as a reminder that the institute is a tenant, whose safety depends on a landlord whose priorities are not always the same as its own. The repair was completed in November.

Instrument booking is handled through a shared calendar, and Brandt reviews usage statistics every quarter. The data show that the electron microscope at TUM, which NIAM uses by arrangement, is booked far more heavily than any instrument on the institute's own floors, and that the characterisation hall's X-ray diffractometer stands idle on many afternoons. Brandt has proposed a policy under which unused slots are released to other groups twenty-four hours in advance, and the steering meeting approved it on a trial basis. Early results suggest that utilisation of the diffractometer has risen noticeably.

Further investment in the building is planned. The roof of the basement is to be reinforced to support a heavier magnet that the institute hopes to install, and the lighting in two laboratories will be replaced by more efficient fittings. Brandt has also begun to prepare the application for a safety certification that several funders now require. He anticipates that the certification will take about a year and will demand better documentation than the institute currently keeps, and he has asked each group head to nominate a safety delegate who will help to collect it.

In November, the cryostat controller in the low-temperature laboratory failed, and the incident offers an instructive example of how the institute's procedures work in practice. The controller regulates the temperature of the main measurement cryostat, and a failure during a cooldown can ruin days of preparation and waste a large quantity of liquid helium. The event began at 14:20 on November 19, when the temperature readback on the operators' panel jumped by several kelvin and the alarm sounded. Within a minute, Noor Haddad, who was working in the control room, had confirmed that the cryostat itself was stable but that the controller was no longer responding to commands.

Haddad consulted the operating note that Tomas Ekberg's group had written for the device, which gave a short list of checks. She verified that the controller had power, that the network cable was connected, and that the input-output controller process was running on its server. All three were in order. She then tried to restart the controller, which did not respond, and she concluded that the fault lay in the hardware. At 14:35 she informed Ekberg and Lukas Brandt, who arrived in the laboratory a few minutes later and made sure that nobody was working with cryogenic liquids near the cryostat.

The spare board for the controller was kept on the labelled shelf that Ekberg's group had established earlier in the year. Haddad and Felix Hartmann replaced the failed board in under an hour, loaded the saved configuration from the repository, and restarted the process. At 15:20 the temperature readback returned to normal, and the control loop resumed. The cryostat had drifted upward by a little less than two kelvin during the outage, and the measurement in progress could be continued after a pause of thirty minutes. Dr. Rahul Menon, who owned the sample inside, said afterwards that he had watched the whole episode with a mixture of horror and gratitude.

An investigation followed, led by Ekberg. The failed board was returned to the manufacturer, whose report concluded that a capacitor had degraded, probably as a result of heat. The controller sat in a rack with poor airflow, and the temperature inside the cabinet had been above the manufacturer's recommended limit for months. Nobody had noticed because the cabinet temperature was not monitored. The group therefore added a temperature sensor to the cabinet, connected it to the monitoring dashboard, and set an alarm threshold. It also rearranged the rack so that the controller no longer sits above a power supply that gives off heat.

The investigation also examined the response. The alarm had reached the operators immediately, the diagnostic checklist had worked, the spare board was where it should have been, and the saved configuration allowed a quick restart. Ekberg identified two weaknesses: the on-call phone had not received the alarm because its number had not been updated after a change of staff, and the configuration repository had been two weeks out of date with respect to a minor change made at the device. Both were corrected within days, and the group introduced a monthly check that the saved configuration matches the running one.

The incident also had a human dimension. Haddad, who had been at the institute for only eight months, handled a stressful situation calmly, and several colleagues praised her conduct. Ekberg used the occasion to recognise the importance of documentation, observing that the operating note had saved at least an hour of searching. Brandt added the event to the institute's incident register as a near miss, so that the lessons could be shared with the other groups. He presented a summary at the next steering meeting, with a short timeline and the corrective actions, and the meeting adopted a recommendation to check the temperature of every critical cabinet in the institute.

The cost of the failure was small. The replacement board cost a few hundred euros, the lost measurement time was about ninety minutes, and no helium was wasted. Had the same fault happened at night or over a weekend, with nobody present, the consequences could have been much worse. The group's conclusion was that the institute had been lucky in the timing and prepared in the response, and that preparation is the only reliable way to turn good luck into a habit. The case has since been used in the induction course for new staff as an example of how a procedure, a spare part, and a calm person together prevent a small fault from becoming a large one.

The institute's electricity consumption became a topic of serious discussion this year, partly because of rising prices and partly because the staff themselves began to ask. NIAM consumed about 2.1 gigawatt hours of electricity, a figure that included the furnaces, the cryogenic equipment, the cleanroom ventilation, the computing cluster, and the general building services. Dr. Priya Raman asked a small working group to examine where the energy went and to propose measures that would reduce consumption without damaging the research. The group was chaired by Lukas Brandt and included Martina Keller, Tomas Ekberg, and a doctoral student from each of the other groups.

The first finding was that the furnaces and the cleanroom ventilation together accounted for more than half of the electricity used. The crystal growth group had already retired two old furnaces as a result of the strategic review, and the dismantling in October removed a continuous load of several kilowatts. The refurbished cleanroom, with its more efficient ventilation, saves roughly a third of the energy that the old system used. Anika Voss pointed out that a furnace that runs for three weeks to grow a single useful crystal is not wasteful if the crystal enables ten publications, but she agreed that a furnace left idling at temperature over a holiday certainly is.

The computing cluster was the second area. Jonas Keller's group trained its models at night and in blocks, which allowed the cluster to be switched into a low-power state during the day when it was not needed. Hanna Lindqvist measured the energy used by her knowledge graph pipeline and found that most of it went into the language model calls that were repeated unnecessarily: documents whose text had not changed were being processed again. Adding a cache that stores the extraction result for each chunk removed those repeats and cut the cost of a typical run considerably. The group concluded that careful engineering can save more energy than a faster processor.

Helium, which the institute uses in large quantities to cool its magnets and cryostats, is a scarce and expensive resource. Until this year, the helium that boiled off during measurements escaped into the atmosphere. The new cryostat in the low-temperature laboratory is connected to a recovery line that captures the gas, and a liquefier in the basement turns it back into liquid. Brandt estimates that the recovery system will pay for itself within four years and will remove the institute's dependence on deliveries that arrive unpredictably. The recovery line was one of the reasons for the delay in installing the cryostat, and Brandt accepts that the delay was the price of doing the job properly.

Travel is a smaller part of the institute's footprint but a more visible one. The institute pays for one conference trip per researcher each year, and a growing number of staff chose to travel by train, including a group that took the train to a workshop in Vienna and arrived rested. The steering meeting adopted a policy under which trips below a certain distance must be made by rail unless the traveller can show a good reason otherwise. Martina Keller notes that the policy has so far cost nothing and may save money, since European rail tickets are often cheaper than flights when the time of the traveller is counted.

Waste from the laboratories includes solvents, used crucibles, broken glassware, and electronic equipment. The group arranged for the safe disposal of the old chemicals that had accumulated in storage, and it began to track the volume of each category of waste. Reuse is encouraged where possible: offcuts of crystals are catalogued and used for test measurements, and the control systems group strips old equipment for spare boards and power supplies. Felix Hartmann built a simple inventory of reusable components, which saved the group several hundred euros in its first quarter.

The working group also discussed what it should not do. Some suggestions, such as installing solar panels on the building roof, were rejected because the roof belongs to the university and is already scheduled for repair. Others, such as shifting all experiments to off-peak hours, would have disrupted people's lives for a small saving. The group preferred measures that are cheap, durable, and invisible to the scientists, which is why it focused on ventilation, idle equipment, and the cluster. Raman endorsed this approach and asked for a progress report each year.

By the end of the year, the group had reduced electricity consumption by about eight percent compared with the previous twelve months, despite an increase in the number of staff. It set a target of fifteen percent over three years, to be measured against a baseline that Brandt and Martina Keller defined carefully so that the comparison would not be distorted by changes in the building. The next steps include submetering the major loads so that consumption can be attributed to individual groups, which is expected to change behaviour more effectively than any exhortation.

Elena Marchetti coordinates education and outreach at NIAM. A former secondary school teacher with a degree in physics, she joined the institute three years ago to build the programme that the Hollis Foundation funds, and she has made it one of the institute's most visible activities. Marchetti believes that early contact with a real laboratory can change how young people see science, and she designs events around that belief: nothing she organises involves a lecture that could equally be delivered in a classroom. Visitors come to see, touch, and ask questions, and the doctoral researchers who guide them are chosen as much for their patience as for their scientific achievements.

The centrepiece of the year was the open day in May, which attracted around two hundred visitors, more than twice the number of the previous year. Marchetti prepared a route through the cleanroom viewing gallery, the characterisation hall, and the crystal growth laboratory, with a stop at every station where a researcher showed something that could be understood in two minutes. The most popular demonstration was a levitating magnet above a superconducting disc cooled with liquid nitrogen. Anika Voss grew a salt crystal in a glass jar over the preceding week, so that children could take home a small piece of what they had watched forming, and Tomas Ekberg's group built a simple robot arm that visitors could move with a joystick.

The summer school in July was a week-long course for first-year university students from TUM, UoS, and RTU. Marchetti worked with Jonas Keller to include a day on data analysis, in which participants trained a neural network to recognise handwritten digits and then discussed what it meant for the network to be wrong. Hanna Lindqvist led the session and reported that the students were more interested in the failures than in the successes, which she considers a healthy instinct. Twenty-four students took part, of whom nine have since asked about master's projects at the institute.

School visits form the backbone of the programme. During the year, fourteen classes visited NIAM, ranging from primary school groups to final-year students preparing for their leaving examinations. Marchetti adapts each visit to the age of the group, and she keeps a notebook of questions that children ask, some of which she finds more probing than those in conference sessions. A teacher from the region praised the visits for making abstract topics tangible, and a local newspaper published an article that quoted Marchetti's remarks about curiosity. The article led to a request from a neighbouring school to start a regular partnership.

The institute also offers a seminar series open to retired engineers and interested members of the public throughout the winter. The series ran for eight evenings, and attendance averaged about forty. Topics included the physics of superconductors, the history of the transistor, and a session on how language models work, which was so popular that Keller repeated it in the spring. Marchetti notes that the retired engineers are the most demanding audience, and the most generous: several of them volunteered to help with demonstrations, and one offered a set of antique measuring instruments to the institute.

Teachers are an important audience as well. In March, Marchetti organised a half-day workshop for local teachers, at which Voss and Haddad explained how a crystal is grown and how a motor is controlled, and they received worksheets and kits to take back to their classrooms. The Hollis Foundation covered the cost of the kits. Marchetti plans to extend the workshop in the coming year by offering a loan service, under which teachers can borrow equipment such as a small cryostat demonstrator and a set of magnets for a few weeks.

Feedback is collected after every event and used to reorganise the programme. The open day's feedback was largely positive, but visitors complained about long waits at the levitating magnet and about the lack of seating, and Marchetti addressed both problems for the next occasion by adding a second demonstration and borrowing benches from the university. She also noted that few visitors with disabilities attended, and she has asked Brandt to review the accessibility of the route through the building, including the lift to the viewing gallery.

The Hollis Foundation renewed its grant of 120 thousand euros, spent largely on materials and on the wages of student helpers. Marchetti reports to the foundation twice a year, and she has been asked to share her approach with other institutes. She is cautious about expansion, since the programme depends on the willingness of researchers to give up their time. Dr. Priya Raman therefore asked each group head to allow every researcher a fixed number of hours for outreach, so that the burden is spread fairly and nobody feels that it comes at the expense of their own work.

The European collaborative grant, which began its first full year of activity in January, links NIAM with research groups in Lisbon and Gothenburg. The partnership came about through personal contacts: Dr. Rahul Menon had worked in Lisbon before joining NIAM, and Clara Dubois, who leads a spectroscopy group in Gothenburg, had spent a sabbatical at TUM and met Dr. Anika Voss there. The three groups complement each other. NIAM grows the crystals, Lisbon measures their magnetic properties, and Gothenburg probes their electronic structure with light, and the grant pays for the exchange of samples, people, and data among them.

The first project meeting took place in Lisbon in February, and a second was held at NIAM in September. At each meeting, the partners reviewed progress and agreed on the samples to be exchanged during the coming months. The September meeting was held in the crystal growth laboratory itself, which allowed the visitors to examine the furnaces and to discuss the growth conditions with Voss and Yuki Tanabe. Dr. Priya Raman remarked afterwards that a meeting held beside an instrument is worth three held in a conference room, and the partners agreed to alternate between laboratories in future.

Dubois spent four months at NIAM as a visiting researcher, working with the machine learning group on spectroscopy data. She brought a large archive of measurements from Gothenburg, collected over fifteen years, and Dr. Viktor Sokolov helped her to clean and label it so that a model could be trained on it. The work revealed that some spectra in the archive had been recorded with a different calibration than the metadata claimed, a discovery that required Dubois to trace the original notebooks. She regards the episode as a vindication of the institute's data management policy, and she has proposed adopting a similar policy in her own group.

The relationship with TUM is the institute's closest. Nine doctoral researchers hold joint contracts, and NIAM staff regularly use the electron microscopy facilities in Munich. Samples travel by courier between the two sites, and the logistics are managed by a student assistant who tracks each shipment in a shared table. The agreement was renewed this year for a further five years, with minor changes to the rules for supervision and to the allocation of intellectual property. Raman describes the negotiation as friendly and thorough, with the most time spent on the question of who may publish what.

The agreement with UoS gives NIAM access to a high-field magnet and a set of large instruments in Stuttgart. In return, NIAM provides crystals and the control software that UoS uses on several of its instruments. Tomas Ekberg's group exchanged engineers with the Stuttgart team, and the shared library of IOC templates that resulted is the most tangible product of the exchange. Menon's visit to Stuttgart in the spring led to measurements on doped strontium titanate that could not have been made elsewhere, and the results appeared in one of the year's papers.

RTU is a looser partner. Its engineering faculty sends students to NIAM for summer placements, and Felix Hartmann and Noor Haddad both studied there. Raman has discussed the possibility of a formal agreement with the dean, but the faculty has no research budget that could support it, and the arrangement remains informal. Meanwhile the institute continues to treat RTU as a source of talented young engineers, and Ekberg has offered to give a guest lecture there next spring.

The industrial partnership with Altmark Instruments GmbH is of a different nature. Altmark is a small company that builds precision mechanics for research laboratories, and its engineers visited NIAM twice to inspect the test bench for the new sample stage. The two sides do not always see the project in the same way: Altmark wants a product that it can sell to other customers, whereas NIAM wants a stage that fits its own experiments exactly. Martina Keller negotiated a compromise under which Altmark may sell the stage to others after a delay of one year, and NIAM retains the right to publish. The first joint meeting after the prototype's arrival is scheduled for January.

Raman regards partnerships as the institute's greatest asset and its greatest source of administrative burden. Each agreement has its own reporting rules, its own contacts, and its own rhythm, and the steering meeting recently asked Martina Keller to compile a single table of obligations so that none is forgotten. The table has already revealed two reporting deadlines in the same week of March, which the institute will now handle by assigning each deadline to a named owner well in advance.

Governance at a research institute is mostly about deciding who may decide. At NIAM, authority is divided among the scientific director, the steering meeting, and the board, which is composed of representatives of the founding organisations and two external scientists. The board meets twice a year to approve the budget and the annual plan, to review the director's report, and to decide on major investments. Dr. Priya Raman prepares the board papers with Martina Keller, and she has made a practice of circulating them two weeks in advance, which the external members greatly appreciate.

The steering meeting, held monthly, brings together the director, the group heads, and the head of operations. It decides what is too small for the board and too large for a single group: the allocation of laboratory space, the use of shared equipment, the scheduling of repairs, and the handling of conflicts. Minutes are kept by a rotating secretary and posted on the wiki within two days. Raman admitted that the rotation was introduced after one group head complained that the minutes always recorded the same person's views, and that it had the unexpected effect of improving the quality of the discussion.

A scientific advisory committee of five external members reviewed the institute in June. The committee read the annual report, interviewed staff and students without managers present, and visited the laboratories. Its report was generally positive, praising the cooperation between groups and the quality of the infrastructure, and it made three recommendations. It asked the institute to define more clearly how the machine learning group serves the others, to improve the gender balance in technical roles, and to reduce the dependence on a small number of large grants. The steering meeting adopted all three recommendations, and each was assigned to a named owner.

Research ethics and integrity received more attention than before. The institute adopted a code of good scientific practice that follows the national guidelines, and every new member of staff must sign it. Raman appointed Dr. Jonas Keller as the institute's ombudsperson for the coming two years, with a deputy from the crystal growth group, so that a person who has a concern can approach someone outside their own reporting line. Keller is a reluctant holder of the office, but he accepts that the role is more easily filled by someone who is trusted than by someone who wants it.

The use of language models in research produced a particular debate. Hanna Lindqvist's work depends on them, other groups wanted to use them to draft papers, and the steering meeting had to decide what was acceptable. After discussion, the institute adopted a short rule: tools may be used for drafting and editing, but the authors remain fully responsible for every statement, every reference must be checked against its source, and any substantial use must be disclosed to co-authors. Keller argues that the rule is merely an application of good practice that has always applied to the use of any tool.

Data protection is a minor concern for a materials institute, but it applies to the personal data of staff and to the confidential data supplied by industrial partners. The institute's data protection officer, a university employee, visited in the spring and reviewed the handling of personnel records, the access controls on the file server, and the retention periods. She found the arrangements adequate and recommended that the institute delete interview recordings and application documents of unsuccessful candidates after six months, which Martina Keller has since put into practice.

Conflicts of interest are registered. Any member of staff who has a financial interest in a company that deals with the institute, or who is a relative of a supplier, must declare it, and Brandt maintains the register. The only entries this year concerned a student assistant whose uncle runs a small firm that supplies cables, and the matter was resolved by letting a colleague place the orders. Raman says she would rather have a long register of harmless declarations than a short one that conceals a problem.

Finally, the institute began to review its procedures for handling complaints. A confidential channel exists through the ombudsperson, and a second channel leads to the board, but the advisory committee noted that few staff knew how to use either. The steering meeting agreed to describe both channels in the induction pack and to remind staff of them at the annual assembly. Raman concluded the discussion with a remark that the institute would know the procedures worked only when someone used them, and that it should hope this would happen rarely.

In preparing this report, the editors asked several members of staff to reflect on the year in their own words. The following remarks are condensed from those conversations, and they are published with the speakers' permission. They are not meant to represent the official view of the institute, which is set out elsewhere, but to give the reader a sense of what working at NIAM is like from different positions. The editors chose speakers from different groups and different stages of their careers, and they asked each of them the same question: what surprised you this year?

Yuki Tanabe, the doctoral researcher in the crystal growth group, said that she had been surprised by how much of the work consists of waiting. A furnace takes days to reach temperature, a crystal takes weeks to grow, and a shipment takes a month to clear customs. She said that she had learned to plan several experiments in parallel, so that a delay in one did not stop everything. She added that the best advice she had received was from Dr. Rahul Menon, who told her to keep a logbook with enough detail that she could repeat a run a year later without remembering anything about it.

Noor Haddad, the control engineer, said that the biggest surprise was the quality of the documentation. In her previous job, she explained, documentation was something one wrote after the fact and nobody read. At NIAM, the operating notes are read, updated, and quoted, and they decide whether a failure costs an hour or a day. She described the November incident as the moment she understood why Tomas Ekberg insists on the rule, and said that she had since written notes for three devices that nobody had asked her to document.

Hanna Lindqvist, from the machine learning group, said that she had been surprised by the difficulty of deciding whether two names refer to the same thing. She had expected the language model to be the hard part, but she found that the model could extract facts quite well, whereas deciding that two mentions belong to a single entity required patience, evidence, and a willingness to leave a question open. She now believes that a system which says it does not know is better than one that guesses, and she has changed her pipeline to keep uncertain cases separate and to record the reasons.

Lukas Brandt, who oversees facilities and safety, said that his surprise was how willing people were to follow a rule once they understood the reason. After the nitrogen incident in September, he had expected resistance to the new checklist, but the researchers adopted it immediately and several suggested improvements. He ascribed the response to the way the incident had been handled: no blame was assigned, the cause was explained openly, and the fix was simple. He hopes that the institute will keep this habit of treating mistakes as information.

Dr. Priya Raman, the scientific director, said that she had been surprised by how much the small decisions mattered. The strategic review, which asked each group to name what it would stop doing, had been the most valuable exercise of the year, and the stopping of three modest activities had freed more energy than any new initiative. She said that she would repeat it annually and would ask herself the same question. She also said that she was proud of the way the institute had absorbed the loss of experienced staff without losing its character, and she credited the group heads for that.

Elena Marchetti, the outreach coordinator, said that she had been surprised by the retired engineers who attended the winter seminars. She had expected polite listeners, but they asked difficult questions, brought their own instruments, and offered to help. She now regards them as the institute's most valuable audience and plans to involve them in the teacher workshops. Martina Keller, the finance manager, said only that she had been surprised that anyone found the budget interesting, and then added that she would be happy to explain it to anybody who asked, an offer that the editors recommend readers accept.

For readers who prefer a timeline to a thematic account, this section records the principal events of the year in order. It repeats material from earlier sections, but the sequence shows how the work of the different groups overlapped, and it may help to locate an event that is mentioned only briefly elsewhere. Dates are given as month and day, and all events took place in the reporting year unless stated otherwise.

In January, the board received the strategic statements from the group heads and accepted the three proposals to stop work: the retirement of two older furnaces, the end of three bespoke acquisition programs, and the abandonment of a family of image classifiers. On January 17 the European collaborative grant formally began its first full year, and the partners from Lisbon and Gothenburg joined a video call to agree the schedule. On January 29 Hanna Lindqvist presented the first results of the knowledge graph pipeline at the internal seminar, reporting that it recovered about two thirds of the reference facts.

February brought the first project meeting of the European grant in Lisbon, attended by Dr. Priya Raman, Dr. Anika Voss, and Dr. Rahul Menon. On February 21 the file server began to show errors, and the replacement was ordered on the following day. In the same month the national research grant panel visited, and the institute's group heads gave short presentations. The panel's questions, according to Raman, were sharp but fair, and the institute left with the feeling that the review had gone well.

In March, Noor Haddad started work as a control engineer on March 4, and within days she was writing records for the vacuum system. The file server failed completely on March 12, and the backup was used to restore the data of the machine learning group within a day. Martina Keller completed the first quarter forecast and warned the steering meeting about the timing of the cryostat payments. On March 26 Elena Marchetti held the workshop for local teachers, supported by the Hollis Foundation.

April was quieter. Menon travelled to Munich for six weeks to learn the sample preparation technique, and Yuki Tanabe passed her first-year examination at TUM. The new file server was installed on April 18, and Tomas Ekberg's group tested the restoration procedure by deliberately deleting a directory. Lukas Brandt planned the closure of the cleanroom and informed all users that it would begin in July. In the last week of April, the data management policy was adopted by the steering meeting after two readings.

In May, the open day on May 11 attracted around two hundred visitors, and the levitating magnet was the star of the event. The national grant was formally renewed on May 23, and the news was announced at the staff assembly the following morning. On May 30 the conference paper on the integration of hardware into EPICS was accepted for presentation in Hamburg.

June saw the visit of the scientific advisory committee, which interviewed staff and toured the laboratories on June 9 and 10. On June 14 Tanabe grew her first usable lithium niobate crystal in the optical floating-zone furnace she had built. The first drafts of the annual budget were circulated on June 25, and Raman gave the board a preliminary account of the year.

In July, the cleanroom closed on July 7 for three weeks of ventilation work and reopened on July 28. The summer school for first-year students ran from July 14 to July 18, with twenty-four participants from TUM, UoS, and RTU. Menon's measurements on doped strontium titanate were completed at UoS, and the results were written up in August.

August was a month of holidays and papers, and little else happened beyond routine operations. Several manuscripts were submitted, including the methods article on lithium niobate. On August 20 the first prototype parts for the new sample stage were delivered by Altmark Instruments GmbH, and Felix Hartmann began to assemble them on the test bench.

September brought the second project meeting of the European grant, held at NIAM on September 8 and 9. The nitrogen splash incident occurred on September 16, and Brandt introduced the new checklist within the week. The prototype of the sample stage was completed on September 25. The first measurements showed that thermal drift was larger than the specification allowed.

October included the dismantling of the two retired furnaces on October 6 and the failure of a pressure gauge on the night of October 14, when the operating note proved its worth. The new cryostat arrived in late October after the delay, and the helium recovery line was commissioned in the first week of November. Samuel Achterberg retired on October 31, and the farewell party was held the same afternoon.

In November, the roof above the sample preparation room was repaired after the autumn leak, and the cryostat controller failed on November 19 and was replaced within the hour. December was devoted to the annual report, the preparation of the next year's budget, and a final steering meeting on December 15 at which the group heads agreed on the milestones for the coming period. Raman closed the year with a short message to the staff, thanking them for the care with which they had carried out unglamorous work.

The institute enters the next period from a position of relative strength. The national grant is secured for three years, the European grant has begun well, the cleanroom has been renovated, and the data policy and control framework are in place. Dr. Priya Raman used the final steering meeting of the year to set out the priorities for the coming years, and the following pages summarise her remarks and the discussion they provoked. She emphasised that the priorities are not wishes but commitments, each with a named owner and a date.

The most ambitious goal is a shared graph of the institute's knowledge, which would link the literature, the growth records, the measurements, and the analysis results into a single searchable structure. Dr. Jonas Keller and Hanna Lindqvist are responsible for the project, and they plan to deliver a first version covering the institute's own publications and growth records by the third quarter of next year. Raman acknowledged that the project depends on the quality of the underlying records and on the discipline of the groups that produce them. She asked each group head to nominate a contact who will help to resolve questions about names, units, and sample identifiers.

The second goal is a second crystal growth line, dedicated to samples for TUM and UoS. Dr. Anika Voss estimates that it would raise the group's output by about a third and allow Yuki Tanabe to move on to a more ambitious set of compounds. Funding is not yet secured. Martina Keller will prepare an application for the equipment infrastructure programme of the national funder, due in the spring, and she has asked Voss for a realistic account of the running costs, since the funder will not pay for a furnace that the institute cannot afford to operate.

A third goal is a unified interface to every instrument at the institute, so that data from any device can be collected by the same software. Tomas Ekberg accepts that this will take years, and his immediate priorities are to finish the Altmark sample stage, to retire the last three bespoke acquisition programs, and to train two more people in EPICS. He also proposed to publish the institute's library of IOC templates openly, together with the documentation rules, and the steering meeting supported the proposal.

A joint training programme with TUM and UoS is the fourth goal. The three institutions run separate courses on laboratory methods, scientific computing, and good scientific practice, with considerable overlap. Raman proposed to merge them into a single programme taught alternately at each site, which would save effort and give doctoral students the chance to meet their peers. A working group led by Elena Marchetti and a representative from each university will report to the board in the third quarter, and its recommendations will include a plan for funding the travel of students.

Raman was candid about the risks. Energy prices could rise again and make the furnaces and the cleanroom more expensive to operate. The institute competes for technical staff with industry, which pays better, and it may lose engineers like Noor Haddad if it cannot offer interesting work and reasonable conditions. Delays in the delivery of equipment, which affected the cryostat this year, may recur. And the dependence on a small number of large grants remains a structural weakness that the institute can reduce only slowly. The steering meeting agreed to review the risks annually and to maintain a modest reserve for the unforeseen.

She also reflected on what the institute should not become. NIAM is small by design, and Raman does not want it to grow to the point at which people no longer know each other, or at which administrative procedures outweigh the research they serve. She favours a stable headcount of around seventy, a few new initiatives each year, and a habit of ending projects that have run their course. The strategic review at the start of the year was, in her view, the most valuable exercise of the period, and she asked the group heads to repeat it annually.

The report closes with a list of milestones, each with a named owner and a target date. Among them are the delivery of the first version of the knowledge graph, the decision on the thermal drift of the sample stage, the recruitment of Menon's replacement, the submission of the equipment application, and the publication of the data management policy. Raman thanked the staff for their effort and noted that the year's achievements were collective. The next report, she said, should show measurable progress on every item on the list, and she looked forward to reading it.

The institute is grateful to the funders whose support made the work described in this report possible. The national research grant provided the foundation of the institute's personnel budget, and the European collaborative grant enabled the exchange of samples, people, and data with colleagues in Lisbon and Gothenburg. The Hollis Foundation supported the school outreach programme for the third consecutive year, and Altmark Instruments GmbH contributed mechanical design and manufacturing capacity for the new sample stage. Without these sources of funding, most of the achievements recorded above would not have been possible.

Warm thanks are due to the partner universities. The Technical University of Munich provided joint doctoral contracts, electron microscopy time, and an open door for short research stays. The University of Stuttgart offered access to a high-field magnet and a group of engineers willing to compare notes. Rheinbach Technical University sent students whose energy and competence earned them places in the control systems group. Colleagues at all three institutions gave their time generously, and the institute hopes it has repaid some of that generosity through the crystals and software it supplied in return.

Individuals deserve mention as well. Samuel Achterberg, who retired after nearly thirty years, built much of the institute's physical infrastructure and trained a generation of researchers in the skills that cannot be learned from a manual. Dr. Rahul Menon, who leaves for Lisbon, shaped the institute's data policy and its flux-growth method. The members of the operations group, Martina Keller, Lukas Brandt, and Elena Marchetti, made the work of the others possible and are seldom thanked for it. The student assistants, including Felix Hartmann, did a great deal of necessary work for modest pay and with unfailing good humour.

The institute also thanks the members of its scientific advisory committee, who read a long report and gave honest advice, and the board, whose members engaged seriously with the papers they were sent. Finally, it thanks the technicians who answered the telephone at two in the morning when a pressure gauge failed, and who will, no doubt, do so again.
